Early last year, a client I was staffing engineers for started tracking everyone by pull requests opened per month. It was a spreadsheet: about two dozen names down the side, months across the top, every cell colored red to green. Nobody announced it. It showed up in leadership conversations, and after a couple of months everybody knew it was there, because many of my people were near the bottom of it.

Some of that was real. We shipped bigger PRs than the other teams, and I had been asking for smaller ones for years. Some of it may have been an artifact, since one repo squashed commits on merge and another didn't, and I honestly can't remember anymore how much was the counting and how much was us not breaking work into meaningful chunks. I pointed out the counting problem at the time. It didn't change the color of the cells.

I'm not proud of what I did next. We were contractors, and every so often one of my engineers got singled out as underperforming because of a low PR count. Nobody on the client's leadership was looking at what was in the PRs. The engineer with twelve that month had usually done harder, more sophisticated work than the one with twenty-five, and the spreadsheet had no column for that. So in February I wrote the team a message. We need to start breaking up work into smaller, more frequent PRs where it makes sense, I told them, and "let's position ourselves well while maintaining good engineering practices." A few of them had already figured it out on their own. I was coaching them to compete on the number the client had chosen until we could get that metric replaced with something better, and I'd do it again in that seat.

It worked, for the number. The month after that message, PRs across the whole org, not just my team, went up by more than half over where they'd sat all winter, and stayed up. Part of that was us. Part of it was the client's own engineers finding out about the spreadsheet and doing what engineers do when they learn about a metric that doesn't paint them well. The amount of code being changed didn't move, so the average PR shrank to a bit more than half its old size. Same work, cut into more pieces. Then people started approving one piece of a change without being able to see the rest of it, and the Teams channel picked up a new kind of message: I really need this PR approved so I can get this other one merged. Nobody was cheating. The count kept going up, the work got harder to follow, and some of that mess was mine to own, since I'd asked for it.

## I pushed for DORA and SPACE, and they warn you about exactly this

The same week I sent that message, I pulled the client's PR data myself and added lines of code to it, so the leaderboard would show size and not just count. The file is still in my drive, named "better metrics?", question mark included. Then I pushed for DORA and SPACE, the two frameworks most teams reach for: DORA measures delivery (how often you ship, how fast a change lands, how often it breaks), and SPACE adds how the people doing the work are doing. We'd been running quarterly developer-experience surveys for most of a year by then, which is the SPACE side of it, but the delivery numbers were new to that client. At the time I thought of both as better metrics. I reread them this week and most of what they say is about how not to use metrics.

The [SPACE paper](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124) says productivity ["cannot be reduced to a single dimension (or metric!)"](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124#:~:text=productivity%20cannot%20be%20reduced%20to%20a%20single%20dimension), and it uses our number as the example: counts like ["number of pull requests, commits, or code reviews"](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124#:~:text=Even%20straightforward%20metrics%20such%20as%20number%20of%20pull%20requests) should ["never be used in isolation to make decisions about individual or team productivity"](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124#:~:text=should%20never%20be%20used%20in%20isolation%20to%20make%20decisions). DORA's [own guide](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/) says its numbers are meant for one application or service at a time and lists "one metric to rule them all" as a way to get it wrong. McKinsey, in the 2023 piece that argued [yes, you can measure developer productivity](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/yes-you-can-measure-software-developer-productivity), admits that if you count commits, ["developers may submit smaller changes more frequently as they seek to game the system"](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/yes-you-can-measure-software-developer-productivity#:~:text=developers%20may%20submit%20smaller%20changes%20more%20frequently). That's my team, in a McKinsey article.

DORA and SPACE can be gamed too, and their own guides say so. What the switch got us was five or six numbers instead of one, and when somebody games one of them, the others don't move with it. PR count went up by more than half and lines changed stayed flat, and one number alone would have looked like a team getting faster.

I've been thinking about that spreadsheet because the AI labs just proposed a metric, and it has one reviewer.

## Dario's plan: one outside reviewer, and Anthropic picks it

Dario Amodei's essay, [We Must Pace the Frontier](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier), came out on the 12th. Most of it asks governments for things. The one thing Anthropic commits to on its own is embedded evaluators. An outside organization gets ["ongoing, employee-like access"](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier#:~:text=ongoing%2C%20employee%2Dlike%20access), meaning desks, badges, laptops, and the internal tools, plus a contract that lets it publish what it finds without Anthropic editing it. Anthropic keeps the right to redact security and commercial details, and if a redaction hides something that mattered to the evaluators' conclusions, [they get to say so publicly](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier#:~:text=The%20reviewers%20can%20say%20publicly%20if%20a%20redaction%20removed%20something%20important). Dario's own reason: ["But we are still the ones choosing what to include and omit. Embedded evaluators will change this dynamic."](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier#:~:text=we%20are%20still%20the%20ones%20choosing%20what%20to%20include%20and%20omit) Sam Altman [said OpenAI would do the same](https://x.com/sama/status/2098811563415150910) a couple of hours later.

I was glad to hear it. Some intentional slowing down, and third-party evaluators with badges and a publishing contract, is more than I expected from a lab, and the redaction clause is the best part of it: the evaluator can say out loud that something was hidden.

What bothered me was the reviewer. [METR](https://metr.org) is the only organization the essay names, as an example, and as I write this nobody official has named a second one. Tim Hwang put the problem in [one line the next morning](https://x.com/timhwang/status/2099145338678378958): the evaluator can be "independent, knowledgeable, or sustainably funded. Pick two." The people who know enough to evaluate a frontier model mostly came out of the labs or the network of funders around them, and anyone who didn't still needs to get paid by somebody. Dean Ball, who says he's done more than most to get new evaluator orgs started, [called METR excellent and "not nearly sufficient on its own"](https://x.com/deanwball/status/2099144818165260581), and I agree with both halves. David Sacks, from inside the White House, [told Dario to stop pretending METR is independent](https://x.com/DavidSacks/status/2098973625252708460) of Anthropic's investors and staff, and by Tuesday the New York Post had METR's funders [on the front page](https://nypost.com/2026/09/15/business/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodeis-handpicked-ai-watchdog-has-deep-ties-to-effective-altruism-movement-a-complete-joke/). I don't want to get too political here, but this is also not the administration I'd pick to be dealing with this. Within three days the president had gone from "whoever wins AI wins" to [calling the whole thing a hoax](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/trump-goes-full-hoax-on-ai-existential). Whatever the labs commit to, the government isn't going to be the one checking. That fight is about whether METR is pure, and I don't think that's the question.

## Every reviewer I ever assigned had a conflict

They wanted the PR merged so their own branch could rebase, or they reported to the same lead as the author, or they were friends. I could live with any of that as long as I knew about it and as long as the second reviewer didn't have the same conflict as the first. So I went and read METR's [conflict-of-interest policy](https://metr.org/coi-policy.pdf) to see how they handle theirs.

It's version 1.0, dated August 28, and for what it covers it's good. Anyone working on a risk assessment of a specific company has to disclose equity, employment, family at the company, even gifts over $100. The test they apply is one I'd use: if we published this in full, would a reasonable outsider trust the assessment less? Conflicts they can't mitigate get disclosed in the report. That handles the individual reviewer, and the organization-level rules are real too: no equity in frontier labs, no donations from labs or their employees, no lab people on the board.

Footnote 17 is where the funding comes in. The policy bars donations from frontier labs and their employees, but it "does not prohibit funding from a public grantmaking organization that itself has received funding from frontier AI company employee(s), so long as such employee(s) are uninvolved in grantmaking decisions." That's the path [Kevin Bass traced](https://x.com/kevinnbass/status/2099626156660043891) the same week, in a thread Sacks reposted, and credit to him for doing the tracing: Dustin Moskovitz's early Anthropic stake sits in a foundation, the foundation funds a grantmaker, the grantmaker funds METR. Bass argues that's capture. I read it as a policy that solved the problem in front of it, the individual reviewer, and wrote down the funder problem as an exception instead of a rule. Moskovitz is an investor and a board observer, not an employee, so the employee clause wouldn't reach him anyway. I've written policies with a footnote like that. Somebody always finds it.

So adding evaluators doesn't fix it by itself. On my PR-count team everybody had the same incentive, so nobody was going to flag anybody else's fragments, and honestly, fair enough. Two more evaluators paid from the same pool have the same incentive as the first one. A second evaluator only helps if its money comes from somewhere else and its charter lets it disagree with the first. The closest thing to that on the table is [Hugging Face asking to join the program](https://x.com/ClementDelangue/status/2098790988034580852), which at least brings different money, and as far as I can find nobody has answered them.

## What moving off PR count looks like for a lab

For a lab, the equivalent is a few checks that don't depend on each other, so that gaming one of them shows up in the others the way lines changed exposed the PR count.

The first isn't my idea. Eli Lifland at the AI Futures Project [argued](https://x.com/eli_lifland/status/2098949260108870063) for a limit on inputs, "a requirement to spend 90% of compute on external inference (and not R&D)," which he thinks is "fairly hard to game." Dario's essay says he worries inputs like training compute ["may be more 'gameable'"](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier#:~:text=may%20be%20more%20%E2%80%9Cgameable%E2%80%9D%20than%20external%20behavior) than behavior, and leaves it for the evaluators to work out. I lean toward Lifland, with a condition I'll get to. The number of chips a lab has is checkable from outside the building, through purchase records and power draw, and alignment progress is not. What the chips are doing is an accounting question, and accounting questions have auditors, who are a profession with exams and liability. Alignment evaluation doesn't have that yet.

The evaluator program is the second check, with the redaction clause, funded the way I described above. The third is a capability benchmark the lab reports publicly, and that one has already been used once. Anthropic tracks its own models on something it calls the Anthropic ECI, a fork of Epoch AI's capabilities index run on internal benchmarks, and publishes the chart in every system card. The [Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/0339e6a7c5c7b87f5c07798616dc32c215d14235/Claude%20Fable%205.1%20&%20Claude%20Mythos%205.1%20System%20Card.pdf), dated September 1, puts the newest model right on the trend line and says so: "We do not believe this data point suggests further acceleration," and the jump earlier this year "was a one-time event that shifted the entire trend line upward, rather than a permanent accelerant of the pace of future progress." Eleven days later Dario's essay said that ["since roughly this summer, AI has been advancing drastically faster, driven primarily by AI's growing ability to build the next generation of AI."](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier#:~:text=since%20roughly%20this%20summer%2C%20AI%20has%20been%20advancing%20drastically%20faster) Lifland [spotted it the same evening](https://x.com/eli_lifland/status/2098943681281892756): the claim is "at first glance contradicted by Anthropic's own internal benchmark." As far as I can find, nobody at Anthropic has reconciled the two. Nobody had to trust a reviewer or trace a donation for that. Anthropic published a chart and a sentence, the CEO published a different sentence, and now Anthropic owes an answer.

## Where each check breaks

I don't want to oversell the compute limit. It moves the gaming from "who reviews us" to "what counts as R&D." Synthetic data generation, distillation runs, and evals each have to land on one side of the line, and the lab gets to argue about which. The condition is an auditor with the standing to lose that argument for the lab. If the lab classifies its own workloads, the limit is decoration.

The redaction clause says the evaluator can speak up. Nobody has said what happens after they do. The essay stops there and so does Altman's follow-up.

And the benchmark check only works while the lab keeps publishing the chart. It's in the system cards today because Anthropic chose to put it there, and nothing makes it stay.

## What if other countries don't play along

The thing I still couldn't settle after all this reading was whether any of it matters if other countries don't play ball. They may not be at the frontier today, but they could be, especially if we slow down and they don't. I'm not a China analyst, so I went back to the essay to see what Dario himself says.

He concedes the point up front. Pacing ["will be limited by the lead that US companies have over authoritarian regimes, chiefly the Chinese Communist Party. If we slow down by more than this amount, then (unpaced) CCP-associated projects will pull ahead."](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier#:~:text=will%20be%20limited%20by%20the%20lead%20that%20US%20companies%20have) A real pause, his Level 4, he calls ["unlikely to actually happen any time soon"](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier#:~:text=unlikely%20to%20actually%20happen%20any%20time%20soon) because the incentive to cheat would be enormous. So the essay isn't asking anyone to slow down past the lead, and the one thing Anthropic commits to on its own, the evaluators, is about seeing inside the lab rather than slowing it.

The compute limit is the only one of the three checks that would slow anyone down, and past the lead it takes a treaty. The other two don't cost a step.

## What I'd want to see

I'm off that project now. As I was wrapping up, they were talking about better tools to evaluate the team, so I hope it's gotten better since. My guess is the smaller PRs stuck, which turned out to be the good part. The good part of Dario's plan is the redaction clause. What would make me believe the rest is three things anyone can check from outside: the evaluators named, plural, with their funders, since OpenAI said ["more to share soon"](https://x.com/sama/status/2098811563415150910) on the 12th and hasn't; a compute ratio published, or a reason it can't be; and the benchmark reported on a schedule, so the next time the essay and the chart disagree, someone catches it the way Lifland did.
