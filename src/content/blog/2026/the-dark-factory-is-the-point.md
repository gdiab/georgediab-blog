In February I wrote that "[most teams do not need a 'dark factory.'](https://www.georgediab.com/posts/2026/the-dark-factory#:~:text=Most%20teams%20do%20not%20need%20a%20%22dark%20factory.%22)" Eight months later I have agents merging their own commits on one project, a review gate on another that won't let a model approve its own diff, and a game where the agent wrote 101 of 105 commits and checked its own work by playing it in a headless browser. Last week Lauren Tan ([poteto](https://x.com/poteto), who ships thousands of PRs a month and wrote [pstack](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/tree/main/pstack)) was asked on [Matt Pocock's stream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3115s) whether what she runs is a dark factory. "[It kind of is, yeah, actually.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3115s)" That's my answer now too, and I owe the February post a correction.

Most of that post held up. One sentence didn't, and it was the one carrying the title.

## The parts I'd still sign

The February argument was that [verification, not generation, is the bottleneck](https://www.georgediab.com/posts/2026/the-dark-factory#:~:text=Verification%20is%20still%20the%20bottleneck.), that "[if you want autonomy, you have to pay for evidence](https://www.georgediab.com/posts/2026/the-dark-factory#:~:text=If%20you%20want%20autonomy%2C%20you%20have%20to%20pay%20for%20evidence.)," and that "[no human review](https://www.georgediab.com/posts/2026/the-dark-factory#:~:text=%22no%20human%20review%22%20is%20only%20defensible%20if%20review%20is%20replaced%20with%20something%20stronger)" only makes sense when review is replaced by something stronger than a human skimming a diff. I kept a list of changes that should stay behind mandatory human review: security-sensitive code, migrations, concurrency, compliance, incident playbooks. All of that is what I run today. Every loop I describe below is an evidence system first and an autonomy system second, and the list of things I won't let an agent merge unsupervised hasn't shrunk.

Poteto's version of the same claim, from the stream: "[verification plus the environment. It's the combination of these two things that allow me to step away and say: agents, go off and merge your thing. I'll review it in the morning by looking at my commit history.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3221s)" Same two ingredients I named in February.

## The sentence I got wrong

Here it is: "[They are less helpful when treated as a maturity ladder every team must climb. Different products, risk profiles, and compliance requirements should lead to different stopping points.](https://www.georgediab.com/posts/2026/the-dark-factory#:~:text=They%20are%20less%20helpful%20when%20treated%20as%20a%20maturity%20ladder%20every%20team%20must%20climb.)"

I was talking about [Shapiro's autonomy levels](https://www.danshapiro.com/blog/2026/01/the-five-levels-from-spicy-autocomplete-to-the-software-factory/), and the point felt reasonable: pick the rung that fits your risk profile and stop there. The problem is what happens at a stopping point. A team that decides it's fine at "agent drafts, human reviews" has no reason to build the harness that would let it go further, so it never does. The evidence engine I said every team needed is exactly the thing nobody funds when the lights-off goal is optional. I had the cost right and the sequencing backwards. I wrote "[invest in the bottleneck before pushing for more autonomy](https://www.georgediab.com/posts/2026/the-dark-factory#:~:text=Invest%20in%20the%20bottleneck%20before%20pushing%20for%20more%20autonomy.)." In practice the push for autonomy is what pays for the bottleneck work, because it's the only thing that makes the missing verification visible as a blocker instead of a nice-to-have.

Poteto describes the order that worked for her: "[the big unlock for me for getting to 2,000 PRs is starting from the question and working backwards of: how do I get to the point where my agent can merge its own code?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=2378s)" Start at dark and work backwards. Everything you have to build to get there is the evidence system. If you start at the evidence system and work forwards, you build a backlog item.

So the dark factory is the point after all, in the sense that it's the target that forces the rest into existence. Whether you ever fully turn the lights off is a separate question, and for most of my work the answer is no. More on that below.

## Call it a kitchen

The other thing I'd change about February is the word. Poteto has "[never really liked the term software factory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=680s)," because "[a lot of people when they think factory, they don't necessarily equate that with quality or craft](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=685s)." She calls what she runs a "[Michelin kitchen](https://x.com/poteto/status/2106140831904399851)," and I've landed in the same place. A factory makes people picture mass production. Software is still creative work, and the finished dish still needs someone's judgment before it goes out.

In a kitchen the agents are the cooks, and you divide the stations on purpose so their work comes together into one good dish. The kitchen itself is your engineering environment: sharp knives and mise en place, which for us means skills, tools, code structure, verification, and lint rules. The kitchen decides what the cooks can reliably produce.

And the head chef owns everything that leaves the kitchen. "[You are not writing the code yourself anymore. You have agents. But you as the human are still responsible for the final outcome.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=880s)" A team of cooks doesn't change whose name is on the plate.

That's how I square the kitchen with aiming for dark. Lights-off means the cooks don't need me hovering at every station. It doesn't mean nobody tastes the dish.

## What it looks like when you aim for dark

Four of my own kitchens, with the numbers, because this is the kind of claim that's cheap to make and expensive to show.

Scrapwake is a browser space-arcade game I built with Cursor's cloud agents, driven from a Grok Bot project. Of its 105 commits, 101 are authored by the agent; I wrote three and opened the nine PRs. The verification loop I gave it was simple to describe and took most of the effort: teach the agent how to play the game so it could confirm its own work, and have it take screenshots so I could confirm too. `npm run verify` builds the game, runs 154 unit tests, then runs Playwright smoke tests that drive the real game in headless Chrome through test hooks, with real keyboard events and real collision frames, and saves the screenshots. A coverage test fails the build if any gameplay contract in the table is missing one of its four required cases, so the agent can't quietly skip a scenario. The agent also runs revert checks on its own: break the feature on purpose, confirm the test goes red, restore it. Six of those are documented for one shield mechanic.

The loop lives in a skill the agent maintains called "Verify before done." It opens with "Do not tell the user a change is done, deployed, or ready to play until something has observed the behavior they asked for." Its rules are the ones I'd have written after a decade of code review if I'd been honest about what review was for: "Test hooks only set up state," never re-implement game logic. "Check rendered state, not saved state." "Green reached by deleting a check is a fail, so restore the check and fix the game." And the rule I like most, because it's the one most teams skip: "When the user finds a bug verify missed, treat it as two bugs: the feature bug, and the gap in the checks. Fix both, add a structural guard so that kind of gap can't come back, and add the lesson to this skill." The skill grew every time I found something it hadn't. My job was playtesting the deployed build and sending feedback like "too fat, not cool looking enough." The skill even reserves that for me: "Claiming the game feels good. Feel stays with the user's playtest." That's sampling, and it's the whole of my review.

[Tightbeam](https://georgediab.com/posts/2026/running-an-organization-of-agents-through-tightbeam) is the org of agents I wrote about in September. Its rule is that nobody approves their own code: a Claude coder's "done" is a claim, and the claim is held until a reviewer in a different session, usually a Codex model, files a clean verdict. One branch went through four review rounds before it became a PR. Another took six rounds with Mike's agents. On the first night the org ran on its new machine, a Codex reviewer checking a Claude fix removed the fix, confirmed the gate failed without it, put it back, and only then signed off. Nobody told it to do that. [CC: rework rate goes here once derived from the attests. Shape: "Of N reviewed work items, X% needed a second round." If the number is bad, it stays in.]

[Tackle](https://github.com/gdiab/tackle) is a harness I built in July where Claude reviews a frozen Codex diff against the spec with the author's plan withheld, loops fixes back a bounded number of times, and commits only if the staged diff's hash matches what the reviewer passed. The first time I ran the cross-model pass, Codex found three defects that every Claude reviewer had missed.

The autotrader is where I hold the line hardest, because it moves money. The verification there is a drill: kill the executor between order submit and broker acknowledgement, restart it, and prove there is exactly one broker order, one database row, and one decision marked acked. The evidence files are committed next to the code. The rule in that repo's agent instructions is "a claim without the command and its output is not verification," and it applies to me as much as to the agents.

Different projects, different distance from dark. The game is fully lights-off and I don't lose sleep over it. The trading system will never be, and the drills exist so I can tell the difference.

## Where this breaks

Verifiability of the domain decides everything, and I don't have a trick for domains that can't be verified by running the code. Matt asked poteto about one-way-door PRs, the kind that cause data loss you can't revert. Her answer was the honest one: "[for domains where the work is verifiable, this is easier, and the one-way doors become two-way doors in a sense](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3392s)," and then "[it's a great question that I don't really have the answer to](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3455s)." Neither do I. A game is verifiable because you can play it. A migration is verifiable if you can run it against a copy and diff. A compliance workflow often isn't, and no amount of aiming for dark changes that.

Rework is the other cost, and it's the one Dex Horthy is right to keep pointing at. His line is that "[the lights off factory does not work](https://github.com/humanlayer/advanced-context-engineering-for-coding-agents/blob/main/wsff.md#:~:text=the%20lights%20off%20factory%20does%20not%20work)," and his reason is that a PR needing 20% rework, which he calls generous, is "[both an intellectual burden and an emotional burden on both the submitter and the reviewer](https://github.com/humanlayer/advanced-context-engineering-for-coding-agents/blob/main/wsff.md#:~:text=both%20an%20intellectual%20burden%20and%20an%20emotional%20burden)." He's describing a human reviewer absorbing agent rework, and in that setup he's correct. The loops above move the rework onto a second agent and leave the human sampling. That only works when the second agent can run the code, which brings it back to verifiability.

And the cost in tokens is real. Poteto's full autopilot spawns verifier agents that click through the app looking for regressions and fix what they find, and she says plainly it's "[quite token intensive](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3206s)." Tightbeam's four- and six-round branches were not cheap either. [CC: do we have a cost number for either Tightbeam branch? cc-session-cost or the attest ledger might have it. ## The review you do becomes a lint rule, not a comment

The part of this that carries over from my own setups to engineering teams is what review turns into once you're sampling instead of blocking. A head chef can't taste every dish, and after the second or third kitchen it isn't close. You don't want to stop tasting altogether, but tasting everything doesn't scale. Poteto: "[You don't want to be in a position where you're not tasting your food ever again. But you also, for scale, you cannot be tasting every single dish that comes out of your kitchen, especially if you have multiple restaurants. So it becomes more about sampling.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=2975s)" This is where the factory earns a little credit back. The quality inspector on a line doesn't check every unit either. They sample, and when something's off they fix the line.

So the practice is to scrutinize a few PRs and look for what your kitchen keeps producing. Poteto's description is the best I've heard: "[you look at the code that the agents are writing and you scrutinize it very rigorously, and you think about all the inefficiencies, the bad patterns, and then you think about how to course-correct the environment. Not that single agent.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3009s)" If several agents take the same shortcut, or keep spreading a pattern you don't want, that's her cue to "[amend your kitchen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=3045s)." The fix is a new skill, a lint rule, or a type constraint, and then the mistake is impossible for everyone, including the next human hire. A PR comment fixes one instance and teaches nobody.

Do that regularly and the stretches where you can step away get longer, because you trust the kitchen is constrained well enough to do the work without you catching its mistakes. That's what Poteto's morning read of the commit history is, and it's what my Scrapwake playtests were: a taste, not an inspection.

That's the management version of the same idea. Poteto, who was at Netflix before Meta, put it as "[context, not control](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=2498s)": you can get the outcome you want by micromanaging, or you can give the engineer enough context to be self-sufficient and stop being in every thread. I spent years saying that about people. It turns out to be the operating instruction for agents too, and the teams that get good at it with agents are going to be the ones that were already good at it with people.

Not full circle. I had the cost right and one sentence about the goal wrong. In February I said "[call it a factory if you want. Operationally, it is an evidence engine.](https://www.georgediab.com/posts/2026/the-dark-factory#:~:text=Call%20it%20a%20factory%20if%20you%20want.%20Operationally%2C%20it%20is%20an%20evidence%20engine.)" I'd keep the second sentence. The first should have said: call it a kitchen, aim for lights-off anyway, and build the evidence engine because that's the only way to get there.

Build one kitchen that produces dependable results, where repeated mistakes turn into changes to the kitchen instead of comments on a plate. Then franchise. Poteto is already there: "[I've spent the time building one kitchen and one restaurant. And now I'm in a position where I don't actually have to be there anymore.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MN9dGgmLyso&t=2040s)" Opening the second restaurant doesn't get me out of the first one. I'm still the one who answers for what goes out of both. Quality is the goal. Throughput is the consequence, and it only shows up once the automation is worth trusting.
